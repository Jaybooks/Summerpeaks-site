A business can be busy, growing, and apparently successful while still allowing valuable profit to disappear. The most dangerous leaks are rarely obvious enough to stop the operation. They are small enough to tolerate, repeat often enough to become expensive, and spread across systems that nobody reviews together.

Use this guide as a diagnostic. Search for a topic, print the checklist, or choose one section. Mark every item as controlled, uncertain, or active. The uncertain items are often where the best investigation begins.

Revenue and Sales Leakage

  1. Completed work never gets invoiced. Jobs, change requests, reimbursable costs, and final milestones can fall between operations and billing unless one person owns the handoff.
  2. Invoices go out days or weeks late. Every billing delay pushes collection later and increases the chance that support, approvals, or customer attention will disappear.
  3. Billable expenses are absorbed. Shipping, materials, travel, permits, platform costs, and outside services are often missed when contracts allow reimbursement.
  4. Scope grows without a price change. Small favors, extra revisions, new deliverables, and expanded service quietly convert profitable work into discounted work.
  5. Discounts are granted without measuring margin. A 10% revenue discount can erase far more than 10% of profit when direct costs do not fall with the price.
  6. Sales credits and refunds lack review. Repeated credits may reveal quality problems, unclear expectations, weak approval, or customer behavior that deserves attention.
  7. Recurring customers receive outdated rates. Long relationships can remain priced for an earlier cost structure even as labor, insurance, software, and supplies rise.
  8. Minimum charges are too low or nonexistent. Tiny jobs can consume setup, travel, communication, billing, and collection effort disproportionate to their revenue.
  9. Deposits are not required for cash-intensive work. The company finances materials and labor while carrying the risk that the customer delays, changes, or cancels.
  10. Sales taxes, fees, or surcharges are absorbed accidentally. Contract wording and invoice setup can turn amounts intended for the customer into company expense.

Pricing and Margin Erosion

  1. Prices are based on competitors instead of economics. Another company may have different labor, purchasing power, capacity, quality, debt, or strategic goals.
  2. Direct labor is priced at wage cost only. Employer taxes, benefits, workers’ compensation, paid time, management, and nonbillable hours raise the true labor cost.
  3. Overhead is absent from pricing. Rent, administration, insurance, technology, and management must ultimately be supported by the contribution from sales.
  4. Estimates ignore normal waste and rework. Scrap, spoilage, callbacks, revisions, breakage, and learning curves belong in realistic cost assumptions.
  5. Rush work is priced like routine work. Expedited jobs disrupt schedules, create overtime, increase freight, and displace other profitable work.
  6. Complex customers pay standard prices. Special reporting, portals, billing rules, meetings, approvals, and service demands create real account-level cost.
  7. Small orders receive large-order economics. Setup, picking, administration, and delivery costs do not shrink proportionately with the invoice.
  8. Quotes remain open after costs change. Long validity periods expose the business to material, labor, freight, or supplier increases it cannot recover.
  9. Profitable and unprofitable services are blended. Strong work can subsidize weak offerings so completely that management never sees which service needs redesign.
  10. Price increases are delayed from fear. Avoiding a thoughtful increase can force the business to accept weaker service, thinner staffing, or deteriorating cash instead.

Billing and Collections

  1. Customer records contain the wrong billing contact. An invoice sent to an inactive employee or incorrect portal can age for weeks before anyone notices.
  2. Purchase-order requirements are discovered after delivery. Missing authorization, job codes, backup, or portal steps can prevent an otherwise valid invoice from entering payment.
  3. Payment terms are vague or inconsistent. Customers exploit uncertainty when contracts, invoices, and verbal promises do not agree.
  4. Late invoices receive late follow-up. The probability of collection generally worsens as balances age and documentation becomes harder to reconstruct.
  5. No one owns accounts receivable. Shared responsibility often means each person assumes someone else sent the reminder or resolved the dispute.
  6. Collections stop after one email. Effective AR requires a documented cadence, multiple contact paths, recorded promises, and clear escalation.
  7. Payment methods create unnecessary friction. Checks, confusing instructions, limited options, or broken links give customers another reason to delay.
  8. Credit is extended without review. Large limits and generous terms can turn a sales win into an unsecured financing commitment.
  9. Unapplied cash hides collection progress. Deposits that are not matched correctly distort aging, trigger incorrect outreach, and waste staff time.
  10. Bad debts are treated as isolated events. Patterns by salesperson, customer type, contract, location, or service may reveal a preventable credit or delivery problem.

Purchasing and Accounts Payable

  1. Purchases happen outside approved channels. Uncoordinated buying reduces negotiating power, duplicates inventory, and makes commitments invisible.
  2. Vendor prices are never benchmarked. Familiarity can replace competition long after rates, service, or terms stop being favorable.
  3. Rush shipping becomes routine. Poor planning converts preventable timing problems into premium freight and emergency purchasing.
  4. Quantity discounts create excess stock. A lower unit price is not savings when inventory expires, becomes obsolete, occupies space, or traps cash.
  5. Duplicate invoices are paid. Different invoice formats, revised copies, card charges, and multiple submission paths can bypass basic duplicate checks.
  6. Vendor credits are never applied. Returns, shortages, rebates, and pricing corrections can sit outside the payable workflow until forgotten.
  7. Early-payment discounts are ignored. When cash is available, a meaningful discount may provide a stronger return than leaving funds idle.
  8. Bills are paid earlier than necessary. Automatic payment without cash planning can surrender valuable liquidity before it is required.
  9. Late fees and service interruptions recur. Missed approvals and poor bill routing create avoidable cost and weaken vendor relationships.
  10. Supplier concentration is invisible. Dependence on one vendor can lead to unfavorable pricing, disruption, and expensive last-minute substitutes.

Labor and Payroll

  1. Schedules are built without demand data. Overstaffing wastes labor while understaffing creates overtime, errors, lost sales, and burnout.
  2. Overtime is reviewed only after payroll. By then the operational cause—coverage, rework, poor scheduling, or capacity—has already repeated.
  3. Employees spend skilled time on low-value administration. Manual entry, document chasing, and repetitive reporting consume capacity that customers would value more.
  4. Time is not connected to jobs or customers. Management cannot see which work consumes labor or whether pricing covers delivery effort.
  5. Payroll reports are not reconciled. Duplicate deductions, incorrect rates, terminated employees, tax differences, and benefit errors can persist unnoticed.
  6. Turnover costs are excluded from decisions. Recruiting, training, lost productivity, mistakes, management time, and vacancy coverage make churn expensive.
  7. Meetings lack a decision or agenda. Ten people in a weekly hour-long meeting consume more than ten hours of paid capacity.
  8. Incentives reward revenue without margin or collection. Sales compensation can encourage discounts, weak customers, or deals that never become cash.
  9. Contractors are used without comparing total economics. Premium rates, rework, coordination, and knowledge loss may outweigh apparent flexibility.
  10. Owners perform work another person could handle well. The hidden cost is not only time; it is the selling, planning, leadership, and analysis that does not happen.

Inventory, Equipment, and Assets

  1. Inventory records do not match physical stock. Purchasing and sales decisions then rely on quantities that do not exist.
  2. Slow-moving inventory is not identified. Aging stock ties up cash, occupies space, and often requires deeper discounts later.
  3. Shrinkage is buried in cost of sales. Theft, damage, spoilage, and counting errors need visibility before management can reduce them.
  4. Stockouts lose both the sale and the customer. Repeated unavailability can shift future demand to a competitor permanently.
  5. Reorder points ignore supplier lead times. Emergency orders and production delays follow when replenishment logic is based on intuition.
  6. Returns are not connected to products or causes. Without reason codes, the business cannot distinguish quality, fulfillment, description, or customer-expectation problems.
  7. Equipment is replaced before utilization is understood. Idle capacity, poor scheduling, or maintenance issues may be the real constraint.
  8. Preventive maintenance is deferred. Small planned costs become major repairs, downtime, missed deadlines, and premium replacements.
  9. Unused assets remain insured, stored, and depreciating. Selling idle equipment can release cash and eliminate ongoing carrying cost.
  10. Inventory profitability ignores marketplace and fulfillment fees. Gross selling price can look attractive while transaction-level contribution is weak.

Operations, Technology, and Workflow

  1. The same information is entered repeatedly. Duplicate entry consumes time and creates conflicting versions of customer, vendor, order, and financial data.
  2. Manual processes have no checklist. Important steps depend on memory, increasing omissions, rework, and training time.
  3. Automation is layered onto a broken workflow. It accelerates poor logic and produces errors at greater scale.
  4. Software licenses are not reviewed. Former employees, duplicate tools, unused seats, and overlapping features create silent recurring expense.
  5. Annual renewals arrive without an owner. Automatic increases and unwanted commitments pass because nobody has time to evaluate them.
  6. Data does not move between systems cleanly. Manual exports, formatting, and reconciliations consume labor every reporting cycle.
  7. Errors are fixed but root causes remain. Correcting the transaction without changing the workflow guarantees another correction later.
  8. Approvals wait in individual inboxes. Purchasing, billing, payment, and customer work stall when status is not visible.
  9. Performance reports require a monthly rebuild. Fragile spreadsheets delay insight and make every management question expensive to answer.
  10. Security incidents are treated as an IT-only risk. Fraud, downtime, recovery, customer loss, and notification costs are financial consequences.

Controls and Financial Accuracy

  1. Bank accounts are not reconciled promptly. Missing, duplicate, fraudulent, and misclassified transactions remain hidden behind an apparently plausible balance.
  2. Balance-sheet accounts are ignored. Old receivables, negative liabilities, stale deposits, and unsupported balances distort the entire financial picture.
  3. One person controls vendor setup and payment. Weak separation makes fraud or error harder to prevent and detect.
  4. Bank-detail changes are accepted by email alone. A compromised account can redirect a legitimate payment to a criminal.
  5. Owners mix business and personal activity. Cleanup time rises, reports become less credible, and business performance is harder to interpret.
  6. Loan payments are posted entirely as expense. Principal, interest, and fees require different treatment; incorrect posting distorts profit and debt.
  7. Merchant deposits are recorded as revenue at net. Fees, refunds, taxes, tips, and timing differences disappear when the payout is treated as the sale.
  8. Payroll liabilities are not reconciled. Tax, benefit, retirement, and deduction differences can accumulate into serious obligations.
  9. Old periods change without review. Silent edits undermine comparisons, reporting credibility, and confidence in prior decisions.
  10. Financial statements are accepted without questions. A report can be mathematically balanced and still fail to reflect the business reality.

Cash, Debt, and Capital

  1. The bank balance is used as the profit report. Cash may include borrowed money, unpaid obligations, customer deposits, or amounts needed for payroll and taxes.
  2. No short-term cash forecast exists. Management discovers shortages only when payment choices are already urgent.
  3. Optimistic collection dates support firm commitments. Vendor and hiring decisions become fragile when uncertain receipts are treated as guaranteed cash.
  4. Debt is compared by payment instead of total cost. Fees, repayment frequency, term, collateral, guarantees, and effective cost can make a small payment expensive.
  5. Credit lines fund permanent losses. A temporary working-capital tool becomes structural debt when the balance never returns toward zero.
  6. Excess cash sits idle while expensive debt remains. Liquidity needs matter, but the cost of holding cash should be compared with interest and risk.
  7. Owner distributions ignore upcoming obligations. Cash leaves before payroll, taxes, annual renewals, or seasonal purchases become visible.
  8. Capital purchases lack a return analysis. Attractive equipment, space, or technology may not produce enough incremental cash to justify its cost.
  9. Financing is sought before the books are reliable. The business may borrow the wrong amount, accept weak terms, or fail to explain repayment capacity.
  10. Growth is assumed to finance itself. More sales often require inventory, payroll, marketing, and delivery cash before customers pay.

Management, Measurement, and Growth

  1. Revenue is celebrated without measuring contribution. Sales growth can hide falling gross margin, higher acquisition cost, and weaker cash conversion.
  2. Budgets exist but variances are not investigated. A plan has little value when actual results never change decisions.
  3. Every customer receives the same service model. High-maintenance accounts consume resources that standard pricing may not cover.
  4. Customer concentration is tolerated without a plan. One lost account can remove revenue faster than costs can be reduced.
  5. Unprofitable offerings remain from habit. Legacy services absorb attention and complexity even when stronger opportunities exist.
  6. Expansion happens before processes are repeatable. A second location, larger team, or new channel multiplies every unresolved weakness.
  7. Management reports contain numbers but no owners. Problems persist when no person, deadline, or next action accompanies the insight.
  8. Decisions are made from stale data. By the time late reports reveal the issue, pricing, staffing, and cash consequences may already be locked in.
  9. The owner remains the only operating system. Decisions, approvals, documents, and customer knowledge bottleneck around one person and limit growth.
  10. No one asks where the next dollar of profit is leaking. Continuous improvement needs a regular financial and operational review, not a once-a-year cleanup.
Busy is not the same as profitable.

A steady footing comes from connecting the work, the cash, and the financial record closely enough to see which activities create value—and which merely create motion.

How to Turn This List Into Recovered Profit

Do not attempt to fix all 100 items at once. Select the five that combine high financial impact, frequent occurrence, and practical control. Assign an owner, define the evidence that will prove the leak is closed, and measure the result for 30 to 90 days.

Then strengthen the accounting system underneath the process. Reconciled records, meaningful account structure, timely reporting, customer and job visibility, disciplined AP and AR, and a credible cash forecast make profit leaks easier to find before they become permanent.
