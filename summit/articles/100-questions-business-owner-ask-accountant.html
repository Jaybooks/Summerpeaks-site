THE OWNER’S ACCOUNTING CHECKLIST

100 Questions Every Business Owner Should Ask Their Accountant

The right questions turn accounting from a compliance exercise into a management advantage. Use this list to uncover weak controls, improve cash visibility, understand profit, and build a stronger financial system.

You do not need to ask all 100 questions in one meeting. Start with the areas where the business feels least clear. A strong accountant should explain what the numbers show, what remains uncertain, and what action deserves attention.

Financial Accuracy and the Monthly Close

  1. Are all bank and credit-card accounts reconciled? Ask which statement dates have been completed and what remains open.
  2. How long does our monthly close take? A defined deadline makes reporting dependable.
  3. Which accounts require estimates or judgment? Know where the numbers are less exact.
  4. What unreconciled differences remain? Small unexplained balances can hide larger process problems.
  5. Are prior periods being changed? Understand who may reopen closed months and why.
  6. What supporting schedules should agree with the ledger? Loans, payroll, inventory, fixed assets, AR, and AP often need separate support.
  7. How are unusual transactions reviewed? Exceptions deserve more than routine categorization.
  8. Are recurring entries still accurate? Old assumptions can continue posting long after circumstances change.
  9. What information do you repeatedly wait for? Recurring delays point to a broken handoff.
  10. What is the biggest reliability risk in our books today? Force the most important weakness into the open.

Profitability and Performance

  1. What is our true gross margin? Confirm that direct costs are classified consistently.
  2. Why did profit change this month? Separate volume, price, mix, cost, and timing effects.
  3. Which products or services are most profitable? Revenue alone cannot answer this.
  4. Which customers or projects may be unprofitable? Large accounts can consume disproportionate resources.
  5. Are we pricing to cover direct cost and overhead? Growth without adequate contribution can increase losses.
  6. Which expenses are growing faster than revenue? Trend review can catch problems before they become structural.
  7. What costs are fixed, variable, or discretionary? This distinction improves planning.
  8. Where are margins leaking? Look for discounts, rework, waste, fees, overtime, returns, and scope creep.
  9. What is our break-even point? Owners should know the sales level required to cover the cost structure.
  10. Which performance change requires action now? Reporting should produce priorities, not just observations.

Cash Flow and Liquidity

  1. Why is profit different from cash? Receivables, payables, inventory, debt, and capital spending often explain the gap.
  2. How many weeks of operating cash do we have? Measure resilience against normal obligations.
  3. What is our lowest projected cash point? A forecast should show both timing and severity.
  4. Can we fund the next two payrolls? Protect payroll before discretionary payments.
  5. Which expected receipts are least reliable? Forecast collections using customer behavior, not hope.
  6. What large withdrawals are coming? Include taxes, benefits, insurance, debt, renewals, and annual payments.
  7. How much cash is committed but not yet paid? The bank balance alone can overstate availability.
  8. What minimum cash reserve should we maintain? Define the threshold before a crisis.
  9. What actions would improve cash within 30 days? Focus on collections, billing, purchasing, deposits, and payment timing.
  10. Do we need a 13-week cash forecast? Businesses with timing pressure usually benefit from one.

Accounts Receivable and Collections

  1. How much AR is current, 30, 60, 90, and 120-plus days old? Aging reveals collection risk.
  2. Who owns each overdue balance? Every material invoice needs an action owner.
  3. Which customers have broken promises to pay? Repeated delays require stronger escalation.
  4. Are invoices going out immediately and accurately? Billing errors and delays become cash delays.
  5. Do invoices clearly state terms and payment methods? Remove preventable friction.
  6. What is our average collection time? Track the trend, not just total AR.
  7. Which disputes are blocking payment? Separate service problems from routine lateness.
  8. Should any customers move to deposits or prepayment? Credit terms should reflect risk.
  9. Are we applying payments correctly? Unapplied cash can distort both AR and customer communication.
  10. What would reduce our collection cycle by one week? A small timing improvement can release meaningful cash.

Accounts Payable and Spending

  1. Does AP include every known obligation? Missing bills make cash and profit look better than reality.
  2. Which vendors are critical to operations? Payment priorities should reflect consequences.
  3. Are duplicate bills or payments being detected? Strong processes compare vendor, invoice number, amount, and support.
  4. Who can create or change a vendor? Vendor changes are a major control point.
  5. How are bills approved? Approval should be documented before payment.
  6. Are we paying earlier than necessary? Protect cash without damaging vendor relationships.
  7. Which vendor terms could be renegotiated? Better timing may reduce working-capital pressure.
  8. Which subscriptions or recurring charges should be reviewed? Small costs accumulate quietly.
  9. Are expenses attached to the right customer, job, or department? Profitability depends on correct attribution.
  10. What payments require my decision this week? Accounting should create a prioritized queue.

Payroll and People Costs

  1. What is our fully burdened labor cost? Wages alone omit employer taxes, benefits, insurance, and other costs.
  2. Is payroll reconciled to the books and bank? Reports, liabilities, and withdrawals should agree.
  3. Are payroll taxes and benefit liabilities current? Unpaid or misposted obligations can become serious.
  4. How much has labor cost changed relative to revenue? Watch productivity and staffing leverage.
  5. Are employees and contractors classified consistently? The financial system should reflect the actual arrangement.
  6. Can labor be connected to jobs, customers, or services? This improves pricing and profitability analysis.
  7. What overtime pattern requires attention? Overtime can reveal scheduling or capacity problems.
  8. What cash must be reserved for bonuses, commissions, or paid leave? Anticipate nonroutine people costs.
  9. Are reimbursements supported and reviewed? Consistency protects both records and policy.
  10. Can we afford the next planned hire? Model total cost, ramp time, and downside—not salary alone.

Reporting and Decision Support

  1. Which reports should I review monthly? Build a focused management package.
  2. What does the balance sheet say about our risk? Owners often overlook working capital, debt, and accumulated balances.
  3. Which numbers deserve weekly review? Cash, collections, sales, labor, and other fast-moving drivers may need a shorter cycle.
  4. Are reports delivered soon enough to change a decision? Speed matters.
  5. Can we compare actual performance with budget or forecast? Variance creates accountability.
  6. What leading indicators should we track? Pipeline, backlog, bookings, utilization, or order volume may signal the future.
  7. What is our most important financial KPI? Prioritize the measure most connected to the current constraint.
  8. Which report is missing today? Identify the decision that still relies on intuition.
  9. Can reporting drill down without losing consistency? Customer, job, service, and location views should reconcile to the whole.
  10. What story do the numbers tell this month? Ask for a concise management interpretation.

Controls, Risk, and Security

  1. Who can access financial systems and bank accounts? Remove unnecessary or outdated access.
  2. Can one person create a vendor and release payment? Separate critical steps where practical.
  3. How are bank-detail changes verified? Independent confirmation reduces fraud risk.
  4. Who reviews bank statements independently? Owner or management review remains valuable.
  5. Are payment approvals preserved? A clear audit trail protects the business.
  6. How are sensitive documents transmitted and stored? Convenience should not override confidentiality.
  7. What happens if the usual accounting person is unavailable? Documented continuity matters.
  8. Are backups and recovery procedures adequate? Know how financial information can be restored.
  9. Which control weakness worries you most? Ask directly for the highest-risk gap.
  10. What fraud or error could occur without being noticed? Design review around plausible failure modes.

Systems, Workflow, and Automation

  1. Where is the same information entered more than once? Duplicate entry wastes time and increases error risk.
  2. Which recurring task is most manual? Frequency makes small inefficiencies expensive.
  3. What should be standardized before it is automated? Automation magnifies the process underneath it.
  4. Where do documents or approvals get stuck? Handoffs often create the delay.
  5. Which systems should exchange information? Integration should eliminate purposeful work, not control.
  6. What exception still requires human judgment? Keep review where risk or ambiguity is meaningful.
  7. Are spreadsheet inputs and formulas controlled? Critical models need ownership and review.
  8. Can the accounting process scale with growth? Identify the next capacity constraint early.
  9. Which process depends too heavily on one person? Reduce single-person knowledge risk.
  10. What one workflow change would save the most time? Start automation where value is measurable.

Growth, Financing, and the Future

  1. Can the business afford its growth plan? Growth consumes working capital before it produces cash.
  2. How much capital will expansion require? Include operating losses, hiring, inventory, systems, and contingency.
  3. Should we use cash, debt, or equity? Compare repayment, dilution, risk, and flexibility.
  4. What financial information will a lender or investor expect? Prepare before urgency weakens negotiating power.
  5. What happens in the downside case? Stress-test lower revenue, slower collections, and higher costs.
  6. Which customer, vendor, or revenue concentration creates risk? Dependence can threaten otherwise healthy results.
  7. What should we stop doing? Better financial management includes subtraction.
  8. What decision are we avoiding because the numbers are unclear? Make the accounting gap concrete.
  9. What should the accounting system deliver one year from now? Build toward the next stage deliberately.
  10. If this were your business, what would you address first? End with a clear recommendation and next action.
Do not settle for a stack of reports.

The real value is the conversation they create: what changed, why it changed, what is at risk, and what management should do next.

How to Use This List

Choose ten questions that match the company’s biggest current constraint. Record the answer, evidence, owner, and next action. Repeat the exercise quarterly as the business changes. The goal is not to interrogate the accountant; it is to create a stronger working relationship and a clearer operating system.

If too many answers are “we do not know,” the problem may not be the individual report. It may be the underlying bookkeeping, close, workflow, or account structure. That is valuable information too: it identifies where the next improvement should begin.
