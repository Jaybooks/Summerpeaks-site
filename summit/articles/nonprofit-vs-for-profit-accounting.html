Nonprofit vs. For-Profit Accounting: Key Differences

A practical comparison for organization leaders and business owners who want to understand the accounting differences.

By Summer Peaks8 min readPublished October 16, 2026

Nonprofit and for-profit organizations share the same need for accurate records, reliable reconciliations and useful financial reporting. But a nonprofit accountant also needs to understand where resources came from, whether the provider placed restrictions on them and how the organization reports its mission-related activities.

The central difference

A for-profit company typically reports performance for owners and investors. A nonprofit must show how it uses resources to pursue its mission and whether donor-imposed restrictions are respected. A nonprofit can earn a surplus and still be a nonprofit; it generally cannot distribute residual earnings to private owners.

Net assets replace owners’ equity

Under US nonprofit reporting rules, net assets are classified as with donor restrictions or without donor restrictions. A board’s internal designation of reserves is not the same as a donor restriction. The restriction depends on the donor’s instructions, not on which bank account holds the cash.

Contributions are not always ordinary revenue

A donation may be unrestricted, restricted to a program or time period, or subject to a substantive condition that must be satisfied before contribution revenue is recognized. Grants require analysis: some are contributions, while others are exchange transactions. A government grant is not automatically contribution revenue, and a reimbursement arrangement does not automatically make it an exchange transaction.

Different financial statements

A typical nonprofit presents a statement of financial position, statement of activities, statement of cash flows, and analysis of expenses by both nature and function. The statement of activities shows changes in net assets; the expense analysis distinguishes program services from supporting activities such as management and fundraising. Requirements differ for organizations outside the FASB nonprofit framework.

Restrictions and cash availability are different

Restricted contributions may be recognized as revenue while remaining unavailable for general operations. Conversely, an organization can have significant unrestricted net assets but limited liquid cash. The accountant must track restriction releases, outstanding grant conditions, payables, and liquidity separately.

Functional expenses matter

A single cost, such as a staff salary or occupancy expense, may serve more than one function. Reasonable, consistently applied allocation methods and supporting records are important. Not every expense labeled 'program' automatically qualifies as a program expense.

Taxes and governance

Nonprofit legal formation does not automatically grant federal tax exemption. Many tax-exempt organizations have annual IRS Form 990-series filing obligations, with exceptions based on classification and circumstances. Some unrelated business income can be taxable. State charitable solicitation, employment, sales and other obligations may also apply. Boards need understandable financial reports and documented oversight.

A practical example

Imagine a local historic preservation organization that sells event tickets, receives unrestricted memberships, and accepts a donor contribution earmarked for restoring one building. Event revenue and memberships must be analyzed under their actual terms; the building contribution requires donor-restriction tracking. Management should not treat the restricted contribution as freely available to cover unrelated expenses simply because the deposit appears in the bank.

What to ask before taking on the work

Request the organizing documents and IRS determination letter if applicable; recent financial statements and Form 990 filings; a list of grants, donor agreements and restricted balances; accounting policies; current budget and board reports; and details of payroll, receivables, payables and reporting deadlines. Clarify whether the organization needs ongoing bookkeeping, grant accounting, financial statement preparation, or tax and audit work that calls for specialized professionals.
