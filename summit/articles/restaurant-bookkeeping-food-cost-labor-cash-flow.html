Restaurant Bookkeeping: Food Cost, Labor & Cash Flow

A restaurant can generate thousands of transactions across the point-of-sale system, merchant processor, delivery platforms, bank accounts, payroll, tips, gift cards, and supplier invoices. If those streams are recorded only from bank deposits, gross sales, fees, taxes, refunds, and liabilities can be distorted.

Reconcile Sales to Deposits, Not Deposits to Sales

The daily or periodic POS summary should be the starting point for sales. Map food, beverage, catering, service charges, discounts, comps, refunds, sales tax, tips, and gift-card activity deliberately. Then reconcile the net amount due from each processor to the deposits that clear the bank.

This makes missing payouts, timing differences, chargebacks, and fee changes visible. It also prevents net bank deposits from understating both revenue and processing expense.

One deposit can represent many different things.

Sales, tax, tips, refunds, fees, and prior-day timing differences should not be collapsed into a single revenue number.

Separate Third-Party Delivery Economics

Delivery platforms may combine customer sales, promotions, adjustments, commissions, marketing charges, refunds, and net payouts. Reconcile each platform statement or report to the books. Management should be able to compare delivery-channel revenue with the direct fees and discounts required to produce it.

Make Prime Cost a Weekly Management Metric

Food and beverage cost plus direct labor are commonly treated as prime cost because they drive restaurant economics. Monthly reporting is useful, but a weekly flash view can reveal an unfavorable trend while there is still time to change ordering, scheduling, menu pricing, portion control, or waste practices.

Use Inventory Counts to Explain Food Cost

Purchases are not always the same as the product consumed during a period. Consistent physical counts help calculate usage and reveal waste, spoilage, overportioning, theft, purchasing changes, and timing effects. Count the same categories, at the same time, with the same method.

Reconcile Payroll, Tips, and Related Liabilities

Restaurant payroll can include hourly wages, overtime, salaried management, reported tips, employer taxes, benefits, deductions, and payroll withdrawals that clear separately. Reconcile payroll reports to the general ledger and bank activity. Tip liabilities and payout methods should have clear, repeatable treatment.

Keep Sales Tax and Gift Cards Visible

Sales tax collected is generally not restaurant revenue. Gift-card sales and redemptions also require deliberate tracking because the sale of the card and the delivery of food may occur in different periods. Monthly reconciliations should explain the movement in these liability balances.

Build Vendor Bills Into Cash Planning

Food suppliers, beverage distributors, linen services, rent, utilities, repairs, insurance, software, and payroll follow different schedules. A short cash forecast should show the next payroll, tax withdrawals, critical vendor payments, rent, debt service, and realistic deposit timing. Profitability and near-term liquidity are related but different questions.

Report by Location or Concept When It Changes Decisions

Multi-location operators need consistent account mapping and a shared close process. Location-level reporting can compare sales mix, labor, food cost, occupancy, controllable expenses, and contribution. Shared costs should be allocated consistently enough to support decisions without pretending every allocation is exact.

A Useful Restaurant Reporting Rhythm

  • Daily exception review for missing or unusual deposits.
  • Weekly sales, labor, food purchases, and prime-cost flash.
  • Monthly POS and processor reconciliation.
  • Monthly profit and loss, balance sheet, and cash review.
  • Inventory count and cost-usage analysis.
  • Accounts payable schedule and short-term cash forecast.
  • Location, channel, or concept comparison where relevant.

A good routine is quiet and repeatable. Like a well-run service, the value is in dozens of handoffs working correctly without the owner having to rescue each one.

When Restaurant Books Need Cleanup

Common warning signs include deposits booked directly to sales, delivery platforms recorded only at net, unreconciled sales tax or tips, negative liability balances, unexplained merchant clearing balances, food cost that swings without explanation, and reports delivered too late to guide scheduling or purchasing.

The restaurant owner's weekly scorecard

A practical weekly view can include net sales, labor percentage, food/beverage cost signals, delivery-platform activity, cash balance and large vendor obligations. Weekly operating signals should be reconciled back to monthly accounting rather than becoming a separate version of the truth.
