Gift Card Accounting: Track Sales, Redemptions and Unused Balances

Gift-card cash comes in before the sale is earned. Keep the outstanding customer obligation visible until redemption or another supported resolution.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 25, 2026

A customer buys a $100 gift card. The business receives $100 today, but it has not yet provided the goods or services. If the full amount is treated as today's sales revenue, both the income statement and the amount owed to gift-card holders may be misstated. A simple gift-card register makes that obligation visible.

Record the sale as an obligation

For businesses using accrual accounting, the sale of a gift card generally creates cash and a liability for the value the business still owes the holder. Revenue is generally recognized as the card is redeemed for goods or services, rather than when the card is first sold. The University of Minnesota's accounting procedure describes this treatment: record gift-card proceeds as unearned revenue, track redemptions, and recognize revenue when the customer redeems value.

Keep a register that ties to the ledger

At minimum, record a unique card or certificate identifier, issue date, initial value, redemptions, refunds or adjustments, and remaining balance. Avoid storing unnecessary customer personal information. For each period, reconcile beginning outstanding value plus new cards issued, less redemptions and other properly documented reductions, to the ending outstanding balance. Compare that balance with the gift-card liability in the general ledger.

If a point-of-sale platform produces a gift-card report, confirm whether it includes promotional cards, bonus value, online sales, and cards issued by third parties. Those categories may need different treatment; do not assume every amount shown in a POS gift-card report represents cash received by the business.

Match redemptions to actual sales

When a customer uses part of a card, reduce the outstanding gift-card liability by the amount applied and recognize the appropriate sales revenue for the goods or services delivered. A partially redeemed card should retain its remaining balance. Reconcile redeemed amounts to the daily sales report so the same transaction is not recorded as revenue both when the card was sold and when it was used.

Sales taxes can depend on the jurisdiction, what the card purchases and when tax is imposed. A gift-card sale should not automatically be treated the same way as a taxable merchandise sale. Confirm the applicable state and local rules rather than applying a single universal treatment.

Review unused balances carefully

Some gift cards may never be redeemed. That does not automatically mean the business can move old balances to income. Revenue recognition for expected breakage depends on the applicable accounting standards, reasonable redemption estimates, and whether amounts must be remitted under unclaimed-property laws. Where an entity expects to be entitled to breakage, recognition may follow actual redemption patterns; otherwise recognition may wait until redemption becomes remote, subject to applicable rules.

Keep issuance and redemption dates so any breakage estimate is based on real history. Review state gift-card and unclaimed-property requirements before recognizing unused balances as revenue or removing them from the liability account. The University of Illinois' stored-value-card guidance illustrates why unredeemed balances and legal remittance obligations require separate review.

Put a monthly control in place

  • Reconcile gift-card issuance and redemption reports to cash receipts and recorded sales.
  • Roll forward the outstanding card balance and tie it to the general ledger.
  • Review voids, refunds, promotional cards and unusual adjustments with approval evidence.
  • Investigate negative balances, duplicate redemptions and long-inactive cards.
  • Document the policy for breakage, escheatment and tax review with a qualified adviser.

Gift-card programs can be useful for cash flow and customer retention, but the upfront cash is not the same as earned revenue. A reliable liability schedule makes the business's financial reports more useful and reduces surprises during a close or audit.
