Vendor Onboarding: Verify Details Before the First Payment

Build a repeatable approval and verification trail before a new vendor receives money.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 22, 2026

A new vendor sends an invoice, tax form and bank instructions. It may be tempting to enter everything at once and release payment. That shortcut can create duplicate vendors, tax-reporting gaps or payments to the wrong account. A short onboarding workflow protects both the vendor relationship and the accounting records.

Start with a complete vendor request

Record the legal business name, business purpose, primary contact, mailing address, payment terms and the person requesting the vendor. Collect the contract, purchase order or approved scope when applicable. Before creating a record, search the vendor master for variations of the name and tax identification details to avoid duplicates.

Collect tax documentation securely

Request the appropriate Form W-9 from U.S. vendors when needed for tax reporting. Check that the name and tax classification are legible and consistent with the vendor record. Keep taxpayer identification numbers in a restricted system, not in an open spreadsheet or ordinary email thread. International vendors may require different forms; refer uncertain cases to a qualified tax professional.

Verify bank instructions independently

Do not rely solely on bank information supplied in an invoice or a new email thread. Verify payment instructions using a trusted phone number or established contact channel obtained independently of the request. Record who verified the details, when, and the method used. Apply the same scrutiny to any later request to change bank accounts. Urgency and secrecy are warning signs, not reasons to bypass verification.

Separate setup, approval and payment

Where staffing allows, assign different people to create the vendor, approve the vendor and release the payment. A small business with one bookkeeper can use owner review of new vendors and bank changes, plus a monthly exception report. Require an invoice and approval before payment, and compare them with the contract or purchase order when available.

Keep a usable audit trail

Store the onboarding request, tax form, verified remittance details, approvals and any change history in a controlled location. Limit access to sensitive information. Review inactive and duplicate vendors periodically and investigate payments to recently changed accounts. Document exceptions rather than silently overriding controls.

A practical prepayment checklist

  • Business purpose and authorized requester confirmed
  • Duplicate vendor search completed
  • Required tax documents received and secured
  • Payment terms and supporting agreement recorded
  • Bank instructions independently verified
  • Vendor setup and first payment approved

These steps can be scaled to the size of the business, but every exception should have an accountable owner. For a broader control framework, see Emory University Finance: Accounts Payable and adapt the principles to your own policies and systems.
