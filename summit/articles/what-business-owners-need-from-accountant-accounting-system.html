WHAT OWNERS SHOULD EXPECT

What Business Owners Should Expect From Their Accounting—and 15 Questions to Test It

A practical standard for small-business accounting: accurate books, timely close, cash visibility, useful reporting, clear ownership and fifteen questions that reveal whether the system is helping.

Business owners do not need accounting that merely records history. They need a dependable financial system that explains what happened, shows what needs attention and supports the next decision without creating another job for the owner.

The minimum standard

  • Bank and material balance-sheet accounts are reconciled on a defined schedule.
  • Month-end has a repeatable close process and target completion date.
  • Revenue, direct costs and operating expenses are organized in a way management can understand.
  • AR and AP are visible enough to support collection and payment decisions.
  • Payroll, debt and other recurring liabilities are not hidden in vague balances.
  • Reports are delivered while the information is still useful.
  • Questions produce explanations, not just account codes.

What useful accounting should answer

Are we profitable? Why did margin change? Where is cash going? Who owes us money? What do we owe soon? Which work is strongest? Can we afford the next hire or purchase? What does management need to do before the next close?

15 questions that test the system

  1. When are the books considered closed each month? A real process should have a target, not “whenever we get to it.”
  2. Which accounts are reconciled? Cash is only the beginning; credit cards, loans, processors and other material accounts matter too.
  3. What changed in profit this month—and why? The answer should connect accounting to operations.
  4. What is our short-term cash risk? Historical reports are not a substitute for forward-looking cash visibility.
  5. Which receivables need action this week? AR should lead to a collection plan.
  6. Which bills or commitments are coming before the next payroll? AP should support payment planning.
  7. What balances on the balance sheet look unusual? Good review looks for reasonableness, not only mathematical balance.
  8. Which customers, jobs or services are most profitable? Ask only where the underlying data can support a useful answer.
  9. What manual accounting work are we repeating every month? Repetition is an opportunity to standardize or automate.
  10. Where are we relying on one person or one spreadsheet? Single points of failure deserve documentation and backup.
  11. What accounting issue keeps returning? Recurring cleanup is evidence of a broken upstream process.
  12. What would you change if this were your business? Useful accounting support should produce observations, not silence.
  13. Can we explain the difference between profit and cash this month? The explanation often reveals AR, AP, inventory, debt or capital spending issues.
  14. What decision will these reports help us make? Reports without users or decisions should be simplified.
  15. What is the next accounting capability we will need as we grow? The system should scale before complexity overwhelms it.

What owners do not need

They do not need an unnecessarily complex chart of accounts, dashboards with dozens of decorative metrics, unexplained accounting jargon or a monthly package that arrives too late to matter. Complexity is only valuable when it improves control or decisions.

The test is simple.

If the owner trusts the numbers, understands the important changes and knows what requires action, the accounting system is doing useful management work.
