Lease Register: Keep Equipment and Rental Agreements Visible

A recurring bank payment does not show which asset is covered, when the agreement changes or who must decide whether to renew.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 20, 2026

A business can pay every equipment lease on time and still have an incomplete picture of its commitments. A copier may have moved to another location. A vehicle agreement may be approaching its return deadline. A renewal notice may be due before the last scheduled payment. A lease register connects those facts to the signed documents and the accounting records.

This is not a replacement for a lease-accounting calculation or legal review. It is a working inventory of agreements, their payment requirements and the decisions somebody needs to own. Start with equipment and premises the business uses under a lease or rental arrangement, rather than assuming the bank's recurring-payment list is complete.

Find the agreements, not just the payments

Ask the people responsible for purchasing, facilities and operations for signed agreements, schedules and amendments. Compare their list with recurring supplier payments and expense detail. Include agreements paid annually or through another billing arrangement; they can be absent from a monthly payment search.

Record the business entity named on each agreement and the supplier's legal name. Identify the equipment, serial number or premises reference, its operational location and the person responsible for it. Keep access to the underlying contracts restricted to authorized people. A descriptive asset reference is usually more useful in a shared working list than unnecessary sensitive contract details.

Build a register with four connected sections

  • Identity and evidence: agreement ID, business entity, supplier, asset description, operational location, responsible person and a link to the signed agreement and amendments.
  • Dates and decisions: signing date, date the asset became available for use, contractual start and end dates, renewal or purchase options, notice deadlines and the person assigned to review them. Capture the contract wording rather than guessing that the last payment ends every obligation.
  • Payment terms: payment frequency, scheduled amounts and changes, payment timing, separately identified deposits, incentives and service charges, plus the source document for each term.
  • Accounting review: reporting basis, reviewer, approved classification or treatment, related ledger accounts and the supporting accounting schedule where one is needed. Track unresolved questions instead of filling them with assumptions.

Distinguish the contract's stated dates from the accounting conclusions reached by the reviewer. An option is not automatically an exercised renewal. A payment total is not automatically the balance that belongs in the financial statements.

A simple example: the deadline before the end date

Suppose an equipment agreement requires 24 monthly payments of $600, with no other fixed payments, and requires written notice 60 days before its stated end date. The scheduled payments total $14,400. That arithmetic describes the stated payment stream, not the measurement of a lease liability or the total cost under every possible outcome.

The register should show the asset, the 24-payment schedule, the actual notice deadline and an earlier internal review date. If management wants to return the equipment, somebody must check the agreement's return process and document the decision. Paying the final $600 does not, by itself, establish that the supplier has released the business from every contractual obligation.

Keep payment checking separate from accounting judgment

Use the register to compare billed amounts and cash payments with the agreement. Investigate a changed charge before simply replacing the expected amount. It could represent an authorized amendment, a separately billed service, a deposit, a credit or a billing error.

Financial-statement treatment depends on the reporting framework, the agreement and applicable accounting policies. Do not assume that every payment belongs entirely in rent expense or that every rental arrangement requires the same balance-sheet entry. Have the responsible accountant assess the arrangement and maintain any required recognition, measurement and disclosure schedules. Tax treatment is a separate question.

Review changes with operations each month

Ask whether any equipment arrived, moved, was replaced or was returned; whether an agreement was amended; and whether a renewal or termination decision was made. Update the register only when the change is supported. Retain the previous terms and amendment reference so the reason for a payment change remains traceable.

Reconcile applicable accounting schedules to the ledger as part of the month-end close. Keep refundable deposits identifiable, and refer questions about asset ownership or a final buyout for review rather than automatically adding leased equipment to the owned fixed-asset register.

Give owners a short decision list

A useful summary highlights upcoming notice dates, agreements awaiting accounting review, payment differences and assets whose operational status has changed. Name an owner and next action for each exception. The goal is to connect a contract commitment with a timely decision, not to produce another spreadsheet that nobody updates.

Source and limits

Boston University's leased, loaned or rented equipment guidance requires its departments to report leased equipment and provide its location, price and leasing period. That is a relevant example of connecting physical equipment to administrative records. The university's insurance arrangements, account codes and capitalization rules are specific to its own operations and are not prescribed here for private businesses.

The register structure and hypothetical example above are practical process recommendations, not a universal accounting policy, interpretation of a particular contract or tax advice. Obtain situation-specific accounting and legal review before making classification or contract decisions.
