Business Loan Payments: Separate Principal From Interest

One bank withdrawal can affect both your loan balance and your interest expense. Keep the two connected without treating them as the same thing.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 17, 2026

The bank feed shows a loan payment leaving your business account. That confirms the cash movement, but it does not explain how much reduced the debt. A consistent loan reconciliation connects the payment, the lender's allocation and the balance recorded in your books.

Start with the payment breakdown

For a straightforward business term loan, repayment of principal reduces the loan liability; interest is recorded separately. Do not automatically code the entire withdrawal to interest expense or reduce the liability by the entire payment. Fees, escrow amounts and other charges need their own review rather than being folded into principal by default.

Mississippi State University Extension explains that an amortization schedule separates each scheduled payment into principal and interest and shows the remaining balance. On a standard fixed-rate, fixed-payment amortizing loan, the split changes as principal is repaid. The schedule is a useful reference, not a reason to ignore actual lender activity.

Build a small loan file before setting a recurring rule

For each borrowing arrangement, retain the signed agreement, original funding record, lender statements and current repayment schedule. Record the lender, a non-sensitive internal loan label, opening principal, payment frequency, maturity date and where supporting documents are stored. Give each material loan a separate ledger account or clearly identifiable supporting schedule.

A recurring bank rule can make processing faster, but a fixed interest split may become wrong in the next period. Decide who will verify the allocation before relying on automatic categorization. Keep full account numbers and sensitive loan documents in controlled storage, not in shared tracking notes.

A simple entry with a clear boundary

Suppose a lender confirms that a $1,000 payment contains $800 of principal and $200 of current-period interest, with no fees or other components. If that interest has not already been accrued, the illustrative entry is:

  • Debit loan payable $800.
  • Debit interest expense $200.
  • Credit cash $1,000.

The payment uses $1,000 of cash, but only $800 reduces principal. If the interest was already recorded as a payable, settle that payable instead of expensing the same interest again. This example is deliberately simple; it does not cover capitalized interest, financing-cost amortization or modified debt arrangements.

Reconcile the liability, not just the bank account

Use a separate reconciliation for each loan: opening principal, plus new principal advances and supported principal adjustments, less principal repayments, equals closing principal. Compare that result with the lender's principal balance for the same date. A total payoff quote may include accrued interest or fees and may not equal principal alone.

When balances differ, investigate the transaction rather than posting a balancing adjustment. Look for a payment recorded twice, an extra principal payment, funding entered as revenue, a payment applied in a different period, a fee included in the wrong component, or an outdated schedule. Document the resolution and retain its support.

Know when the original schedule is no longer enough

Variable rates, additional borrowing, payment deferrals, late charges and refinancing can change what belongs in the current entry. Obtain updated lender information when terms or activity change. Do not keep extending an old schedule merely because the cash withdrawal stayed the same.

For a refinanced loan, trace the old balance, new proceeds, payoff and fees separately. Ask the engagement's accounting professional to determine any treatment that depends on the reporting framework or the transaction's terms. The same caution applies when a loan finances an asset under construction or includes unusual financing costs.

Make the review useful to the owner

A monthly loan summary can show closing principal, payments made, interest recorded, upcoming contractual payments and unresolved differences. Separate the cash needed for debt payments from the expense shown in management reports. For broader context, see our balance sheet guide and financial reporting and cash-flow services.

Keep tax deductibility and financial-statement presentation outside an automatic bank rule. Those questions can depend on the facts, reporting basis and applicable requirements. This article describes a general bookkeeping workflow, not lending, tax or legal advice.

Source and scope

Mississippi State University Extension: Paying Off Your Loans: Loan Amortization supports the explanation of payment allocation and repayment schedules. Its agricultural examples illustrate the mechanics; it is not a small-business accounting standard.
