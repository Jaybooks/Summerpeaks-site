Customer Deposits and Advance Payments: A Small Business Bookkeeping Guide

A practical way to record money received before work is complete, keep customer balances clear, and avoid overstating revenue.

By Summer Peaks8 min readPublished September 23, 2026

Receiving a deposit is good for cash flow, but it does not necessarily mean the entire amount has been earned. A customer may pay before a project begins, reserve a service, or fund work that will be delivered in stages. The bookkeeping process should show both the cash received and what the business still owes the customer.

First, identify what the payment represents

A refundable security deposit, a nonrefundable booking fee, a customer prepayment for future services, and a payment against an already issued invoice can require different treatment. Review the customer agreement, refund terms, what has been delivered, and the accounting basis used for your financial statements. The examples below illustrate a common accrual-basis approach to an advance for services; they are not universal tax or financial-reporting rules.

A practical workflow for customer advances

  1. Document the terms before collecting. Record the customer, contract or order, amount, payment date, what the advance covers, and when it will be applied or refunded. Keep the agreement and payment confirmation together.
  2. Record the cash and the remaining obligation. For an accrual-basis advance for services not yet performed, record the receipt in cash and a customer-advance liability rather than automatically recognizing all of it as earned revenue.
  3. Connect the advance to the customer. Use a customer-level deposit register or subledger with the original receipt, amounts applied, refunds, and unapplied balance. An unexplained general-ledger liability is not enough to answer a customer's balance question.
  4. Apply it when the relevant work is delivered. As services are performed or an invoice is issued under the agreed terms, apply the appropriate portion of the advance and recognize revenue under the business's accounting policy. Avoid counting the deposit again as a new cash receipt.
  5. Reconcile monthly. Compare the customer-level register with the customer-advance liability account, open invoices, refunds, and bank receipts. Investigate old unapplied amounts and negative customer balances.
  6. Review exceptions. Flag canceled orders, disputed amounts, partial delivery, refunds, and deposits transferred between projects. Record the reason and approval for each adjustment.

Illustrative journal entries

Assume a customer pays a $2,000 advance for a $5,000 service project. The business uses accrual-basis financial statements, and the full $2,000 relates to work that has not yet been performed. These simplified entries omit sales tax, processing fees, and any other contract-specific considerations.

  • When the advance arrives: debit Cash $2,000; credit Customer Advances (liability) $2,000.
  • When the $5,000 service has been fully performed and invoiced: debit Accounts Receivable $5,000; credit Service Revenue $5,000.
  • When the advance is applied: debit Customer Advances $2,000; credit Accounts Receivable $2,000. The remaining customer receivable is $3,000.
  • When the remaining amount is collected: debit Cash $3,000; credit Accounts Receivable $3,000.

The business has collected $5,000 in total, recognized $5,000 of revenue when earned under the assumed policy, and cleared the $2,000 advance liability. A business that recognizes revenue in stages may apply the advance in stages instead. Do not use these example entries as a substitute for evaluating the actual contract or the accounting framework applicable to the business.

What about cash-basis books and taxes?

Financial-statement presentation and federal or state tax treatment are separate questions. Cash-basis reporting may recognize receipts differently from accrual-basis financial statements, and the tax treatment of advance payments depends on the taxpayer's method and applicable rules. Do not assume a balance-sheet liability automatically defers taxable income. Confirm the treatment for the specific business and payment before preparing returns.

Monthly review checklist

  • Does every bank receipt tie to a customer, invoice, or documented advance?
  • Does the customer-advance register equal the corresponding general-ledger balance?
  • Were deposits applied only once, and are remaining invoice balances correct?
  • Are refunds, cancellations, and aged unapplied deposits assigned for follow-up?
  • Can the owner distinguish collected cash from work already earned and amounts still owed?

Pair the deposit review with receivables follow-up, monthly bank reconciliation, and a short-term cash forecast. Advances may improve today's cash position while funding work the business still needs to deliver.
