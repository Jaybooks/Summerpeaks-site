Intercompany Balances: When One Business Pays for Another

A shared owner does not make two companies' accounting records interchangeable. Track the purpose, the counterparty and what remains to be settled.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 19, 2026

One business pays a supplier bill that belongs to another business under the same ownership. The bank transaction is easy to see. The harder questions are whether the correct company recorded the cost, whether repayment is expected and whether both sets of books tell the same story. Intercompany accounting makes those relationships explicit instead of burying them in a general expense or an unexplained transfer.

Start with the legal entities and the purpose

First identify which legal entity paid and which received the goods, service or funding. Two bank accounts belonging to one company are not the same situation as two separate companies. Record the entity names, transaction date, source document, amount and business purpose before choosing an account.

Do not assume every movement is a loan. A payment could settle an existing payable, reimburse a cost, fund a documented loan or reflect an approved capital transaction. Ownership, agreements and the underlying facts affect classification. Formal loans, forgiveness, capital contributions and cross-border arrangements need situation-specific accounting, tax and legal review.

A simple payment example

Assume Company A pays a $1,000 supplier bill that is entirely Company B's operating expense. Both companies agree B will reimburse A, and no amount was previously recorded. Under that simplified fact pattern, A records the cash payment and a $1,000 amount receivable from B. B records its expense and a $1,000 amount payable to A. A should not also treat B's expense as its own.

If B already recorded the supplier invoice, its accounting must clear that existing supplier payable rather than record the expense a second time. When B reimburses A, both companies clear the corresponding intercompany balance. Repayment is not new sales revenue for A or another copy of the operating expense for B. Different facts, including shared costs or a funding agreement, require a different analysis.

Keep a schedule for each pair of companies

Use clearly identified counterparty accounts or equivalent system dimensions, supported by a transaction-level schedule. A single unexplained balance called “transfers” makes it difficult to identify who owes whom. Keep financing balances distinguishable from routine operating reimbursements.

For a practical working schedule, include the paying company, benefiting company, document reference, original amount, settlements, remaining amount and the account used in each ledger. Add links to supporting evidence and name the person responsible for resolving differences. Limit access to business records to the people who need them.

Compare both sides at the same cutoff

At close, obtain the relevant ledger detail from each company for the same reporting date. Compare A's receivable from B with B's corresponding payable to A. Then match the underlying items, not just the totals: two omitted transactions can leave an apparently matching balance.

Investigate missing entries, duplicates, payments recorded in different periods, charges assigned to the wrong company and disputed allocations. If currencies differ, agree the comparison currency and translation basis before treating a difference as an error. Record the explanation and any supported correction on the appropriate side. Do not use an unsupported balancing adjustment to hide an unresolved difference.

Separate reconciliation from settlement and consolidation

A reconciled balance can still be unpaid. Agree how approved amounts will be settled, who authorizes payment and how the settlement reference will reach both accounting records. Netting several items requires an agreed, documented process; it should not obscure which obligations were cleared.

Consolidated financial reporting is another step. Applicable consolidation rules require elimination of intra-group balances and transactions within the consolidation group. That does not mean an amount owed disappears from the individual companies' records merely because an owner looks at combined totals. Confirm the reporting boundary and accounting requirements with the person responsible for the financial statements. Not every group of commonly owned businesses has the same reporting obligations.

A repeatable owner review

Ask for a short summary showing the amount each company owes or is owed, the oldest unsettled items and the differences still under review. Pair it with the relevant bank reconciliations and month-end close. The useful outcome is a traceable explanation of each balance, not simply a smaller combined number.

Sources and limits

Stanford University's accounting overview distinguishes activity with separate university-owned legal entities from activity between departments. Its object-code guidance separately identifies intercompany receivables, payments and inter-entity debt. Those are useful classification examples, not account codes prescribed for private businesses.

EY's intercompany accounting discussion describes company-level and invoice-level matching. Deloitte's consolidation guidance addresses elimination of intercompany transactions. The workflow and simplified example above are educational guidance, not advice on a particular financing arrangement, tax position or reporting obligation.
