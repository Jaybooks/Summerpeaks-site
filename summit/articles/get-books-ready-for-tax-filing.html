How to Get Your Books Ready for Tax Filing: A Small Business Checklist

Tax preparation is faster and more dependable when the bookkeeping is already complete, reconciled, supported, and easy to explain.

Tax filing should not begin with a scramble to reconstruct twelve months of activity. The strongest year-end process starts with books that already reflect the business accurately: income is complete, major accounts are reconciled, unusual transactions are explained, and supporting records are organized.

The IRS says business records should clearly show income and expenses and should support the amounts reported on a tax return. That does not mean every business needs the same bookkeeping system. It does mean the numbers should be traceable to reliable records.

1. Reconcile every active bank and credit-card account

Each active bank and credit-card account should be reconciled through year-end. Investigate missing transactions, duplicates, old outstanding items, and unexplained differences rather than forcing a reconciliation with an unsupported adjustment.

2. Confirm that gross income is complete

Review invoices, processor deposits, marketplace sales, cash receipts, retainers, refunds, discounts, and other revenue sources. Deposits are not automatically revenue because they can also include loans, owner contributions, transfers, and other activity.

3. Review expenses for business purpose and classification

Scan major categories for unusual balances, personal items, duplicates, large one-time purchases, and generic classifications. Retain supporting documents such as bills, invoices, receipts, and other records appropriate to the transaction.

4. Separate owner activity from business activity

Owner contributions, draws or distributions, reimbursements, personal purchases, and business expenses paid personally should be identifiable rather than mixed indiscriminately with operating activity.

5. Tie payroll to payroll reports

Use payroll-provider reports and filings rather than only net bank withdrawals. Gross wages, employer taxes, deductions, reimbursements, and benefit-related items can create several accounting components from one payroll run.

6. Review loans, credit lines, and financing

Separate borrowing, principal, interest, and fees correctly. Compare year-end loan balances with lender records when available and investigate differences.

7. Identify fixed-asset purchases

Flag equipment, vehicles, furniture, technology, improvements, and other longer-lived purchases so their accounting and tax treatment can be evaluated using the facts and applicable rules.

8. Review accounts receivable and accounts payable

Year-end balances should represent real outstanding amounts. Look for duplicate invoices, unapplied payments, stale customer balances, old vendor credits, duplicated bills, and settled items that were never cleared.

9. Review inventory when inventory is part of the business

Ensure inventory records support the method being used and capture relevant purchases, returns, damage, and year-end quantities. Accurate underlying records are essential before tax treatment is determined.

10. Clear suspense, uncategorized, and unexplained balances

Temporary categories should not become permanent hiding places. Resolve or clearly document uncategorized activity, suspense accounts, and unexpected balance-sheet items.

11. Organize the supporting documents

Keep invoices, receipts, bills, statements, payroll records, asset documents, and other support organized so important entries can be traced. Retention periods vary by record and circumstance.

12. Perform a final financial-statement reasonableness review

Review annual revenue, major expenses, reconciled cash and card balances, receivables, payables, loans, unusual asset or liability balances, and significant one-time transactions before the books are handed into tax preparation.

The tax-ready test

A reviewer should be able to move from a financial-statement number to the underlying ledger and then to supporting records without reconstructing the year from scratch.

Why year-round bookkeeping makes tax season easier

Resolving issues monthly makes bank differences, missing documents, customer questions, and vendor questions easier to investigate while they are recent. Management also receives better information throughout the year.
