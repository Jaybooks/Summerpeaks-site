Customer Credit Memos: Correct the Invoice, Preserve the Record

When an invoice needs to be reduced, document the reason and approval instead of deleting the transaction or quietly changing the amount.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 21, 2026

A customer returns part of an order. A service charge was billed at the wrong rate. A discount is approved after the invoice was issued. Each situation can reduce what the customer owes, but the accounting record should still explain the original invoice, the change and the remaining balance.

A customer credit memo creates that explanation. It is a separate document that reduces an invoice or customer balance for an approved reason. It should not become a general shortcut for correcting every accounts receivable problem.

Decide whether a credit memo is the right document

Use a credit memo when the business has a supported reason to reduce an amount previously billed, such as a return, a documented billing error, an approved price adjustment or a service concession. Link the credit to the original invoice whenever possible.

Do not use a credit memo merely because a customer paid the wrong invoice, omitted remittance information or sent more than the amount due. Those situations may require payment reapplication, an unapplied-payment review or a refund process. A balance that appears uncollectible also requires a separate decision; it is not automatically a sales adjustment or customer credit.

The distinction matters because the reason for the reduction can affect revenue, inventory, sales tax, commissions, project reporting and management analysis. The correct accounting treatment depends on the underlying transaction, applicable reporting framework and the business's policies.

Require enough support to understand the change

A credit request should identify the customer, original invoice, item or service affected, quantity when relevant, requested amount and reason. Attach the return authorization, approved pricing change, customer correspondence or other evidence. Name the person who requested the credit and the person authorized to approve it.

Approval limits can be scaled to the business. A small routine adjustment may follow one documented approval path, while a larger or unusual credit may require an owner or manager. The important control is that the person entering the credit is not silently creating a discount without review.

Keep the original invoice unchanged after it has been issued. If the business must cancel and replace an invoice under its system or industry rules, retain the cancellation and replacement trail. Editing history away may make the customer statement look cleaner temporarily, but it weakens the explanation for what happened.

Issue and apply the credit in a controlled sequence

  1. Confirm the business reason. Verify the return, billing difference or approved concession against the underlying documents.
  2. Determine the approved amount. Recalculate quantities, rates, tax and any other affected components. Do not copy the customer's requested amount without review.
  3. Create a numbered credit memo. Reference the original invoice and describe the adjustment clearly enough that the customer and reviewer can understand it.
  4. Apply it to the intended invoice or customer account. Avoid leaving the credit unattached when the destination is already known.
  5. Send the document to the customer. An updated statement or credit memo reduces confusion about the remaining amount due.
  6. Retain the approval and support. Connect the request, evidence, credit memo and accounting entry in the transaction audit trail.

A simple example

Assume a business issued a $2,000 invoice for ten units at $200 each. The customer returns one qualifying unit before paying, and the business approves a $200 credit. The credit memo should reference the original invoice and the returned unit. Applying it reduces the open customer balance from $2,000 to $1,800.

That arithmetic does not determine every related entry. The business may also need to address inventory, cost of sales, sales tax, processing fees or commissions according to the facts and its accounting policies. The example demonstrates the customer balance, not a universal journal entry.

If the customer had already paid the full $2,000, issuing the credit would create a customer credit balance rather than an $1,800 receivable. The business would then need to determine whether the amount should be refunded or validly applied to future activity, document the customer's instructions where appropriate and reconcile the eventual settlement.

Review open credits, not just open invoices

An accounts receivable aging can hide operational questions when credits remain unapplied. Review customer accounts with negative balances, old credits, credits without invoice references and credits issued shortly before a write-off. Ask whether each amount is expected to offset a future invoice, be refunded or be corrected.

Reconcile the credit memo listing to the customer subledger and general ledger as part of the month-end close. Review sequential numbering for gaps or duplicates. Compare larger credits with approval records, and confirm that credits related to returns or pricing disputes were reflected consistently in any related operational reports.

Keep true credit memos separate from the allowance and write-off process. A price adjustment changes the amount the business says the customer owes. A write-off addresses a receivable that remains valid but is not expected to be collected. Combining the two can distort both sales adjustments and collection performance.

Use a short monthly exception list

A useful review does not require a complicated dashboard. List credits above the business's review threshold, credits older than the normal resolution period, unapplied customer credits and accounts where both a credit and collection activity occurred. Assign an owner and next action to each exception.

This review can identify recurring billing errors, unclear return practices, delayed approvals or products and services generating unusual adjustments. Those patterns are management questions, not just bookkeeping cleanup.

Source and limits

Indiana University's accounts receivable guidance describes credit memos as adjustments to unpaid invoice balances for reasons including returns and price adjustments, while distinguishing them from invoice write-offs. That distinction supports the process described here. Indiana University's system steps and institutional policies apply to its own operations and are not prescribed here for private businesses.

This article provides a practical accounting workflow, not a universal policy or legal, tax or reporting-framework conclusion. The proper treatment of revenue, tax, inventory, refunds and unclaimed customer balances can vary. Obtain situation-specific accounting, tax and legal review where required.
