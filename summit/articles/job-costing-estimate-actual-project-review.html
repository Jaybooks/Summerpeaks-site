Job Costing: Compare the Estimate With What the Work Actually Cost

Give each job its own cost record so pricing, purchasing and project decisions are based on more than the total monthly profit.

By Summer Peaks6 min readPublished October 13, 2026

A profitable month can hide a losing job. Total revenue and expenses show the result for the business, but they do not automatically show which project absorbed extra labor, materials or subcontractor costs. Job costing creates that missing view by assigning costs to a specific job or service engagement.

Start with a job identifier

Create one consistent identifier before work begins and use it across the estimate, purchase orders, bills, time records, expense reports, change orders, invoices and job-cost report. A customer name alone is often insufficient when that customer has several projects.

Define the start and completion rules too. Without them, costs can drift between jobs or periods, and a report may compare a complete estimate with only part of the actual work.

Separate direct costs from shared costs

Direct costs are amounts that can be traced to a job in a practical way. Depending on the business, they may include job-specific materials, employee labor, subcontractors, permits, freight, equipment rental or travel. The useful categories depend on how the work is performed; every business should not use the same template.

Shared costs require a deliberate policy. Rent, supervision, insurance, shop supplies and equipment use may support several jobs. Some businesses track those costs as period expenses. Others allocate appropriate overhead to jobs using a rate based on labor hours, machine hours, labor dollars or another cost driver. The chosen method should reflect how resources are consumed and be applied consistently. A complicated allocation that no one maintains is not more useful than a simpler, supportable method.

Build the estimate in the same categories

An estimate-to-actual review works only when both sides use comparable categories. If the estimate combines labor and materials into one amount while actual costs are separated, the final variance will reveal that something changed but not what changed.

For each job, keep the approved estimate, approved changes, actual cost and variance side by side. For example, an approved labor estimate of $8,000 and actual labor of $8,900 produces a $900 unfavorable labor variance. That difference is a prompt to investigate—not proof that an employee, customer or price was at fault.

Capture costs while the job is active

  • Require the job identifier on time entries, purchase requests and employee expenses.
  • Code vendor bills and subcontractor invoices to the correct job and cost category.
  • Record approved change orders separately from the original estimate.
  • Review unassigned costs before month-end rather than placing them in a permanent catchall.
  • Reconcile job-cost totals with the general ledger so the project report is not a separate set of unsupported numbers.

Timing matters. A job may appear profitable before a late subcontractor invoice arrives. Keep completed jobs open for a defined closeout review and identify costs that have been incurred but not yet billed.

Review the variance without guessing

When actual cost differs from the estimate, break the difference into causes the records can support. Did the quantity of material change? Did the purchase price change? Were more labor hours used, or was the labor mix different? Was there rework? Did an approved scope change increase both revenue and cost? Did the overhead assumption no longer match the work?

Review favorable variances too. A job that appears unusually profitable may be missing time, materials or a vendor bill. Closing a job before checking completeness can turn missing data into a false success story.

Use the completed job to improve the next decision

A closed job-cost report can inform future estimating, purchasing and scheduling. Compare similar jobs, but keep the circumstances visible: customer requirements, location, staffing, material mix and scope may differ. Historical cost is evidence, not an automatic price.
