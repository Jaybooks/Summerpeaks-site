Construction Bookkeeping: Job Costing, Cash Flow & WIP

A contractor can stay busy and still lose money. The accounting system must show what each job earns, what it consumes, and when the cash will actually arrive.

Construction accounting becomes difficult when the books describe the company but not the jobs. Materials are bought before billing. Crews split time across projects. Subcontractor invoices arrive late. Change orders are approved informally. Retainage delays cash after the work is substantially complete. A normal profit and loss statement can look acceptable while individual jobs quietly miss their targets.

Start With a Job-Cost Structure That People Will Use

Every direct cost should have a consistent route to the right project. The practical categories usually include field labor, materials, subcontractors, equipment, permits, disposal, travel, and other job-specific costs. The category list should be detailed enough to improve estimating without becoming so complicated that coding breaks down.

Job costing works only when the source documents carry the necessary information. Vendor bills, card charges, purchase orders, time records, and reimbursements need a job identifier before they reach the monthly close.

A profitable company can contain unprofitable jobs.

Company-wide results cannot explain whether a margin problem came from estimating, labor productivity, material overruns, scope creep, or slow billing.

Compare Estimate, Committed Cost, Actual Cost, and Billing

A useful job report should not stop at actual expenses. Management benefits from seeing the original estimate, approved changes, committed but not yet invoiced costs, costs recorded to date, invoices issued, cash collected, and the latest expected margin. That gives the business an early-warning system rather than a historical autopsy.

Control Change Orders Before They Become Free Work

Change orders should move through a defined workflow: document the request, price the work, obtain approval, update the job budget, schedule the work, and bill it. When field activity outruns documentation, the company may complete extra work without protecting the margin or the right to collect.

Understand Work in Progress

Longer projects create timing differences between cost, progress, billing, and cash. Work-in-progress reporting can help management compare the financial state of each project with its operational state. The exact accounting treatment depends on the company and its reporting requirements, but the management objective is universal: identify overbilling, underbilling, cost overruns, and margin fade early.

Treat Retainage as Its Own Collection Pipeline

Retainage should not disappear inside total accounts receivable. Track it by customer and job, including the amount withheld, release conditions, expected release date, required documentation, and collection owner. A project is not financially finished just because the crew has left the site.

Build Payroll Into Job Profitability

Field labor is often one of the largest direct costs. Time needs to reach the correct job and, when useful, the correct cost phase. The business should also decide how payroll taxes, benefits, workers’ compensation, and other labor burden affect its internal job-cost view. Ignoring burden can make labor-intensive jobs appear healthier than they are.

Forecast Cash Separately From Profit

Profit does not pay Friday’s payroll if the customer will not pay for another 45 days. A short-term contractor cash forecast should include expected progress billings, collection timing, deposits, payroll, subcontractors, large material purchases, debt payments, tax obligations, and retainage releases. Update expected dates based on actual customer behavior rather than invoice terms alone.

Use a Monthly Contractor Reporting Package

  • Company profit and loss and balance sheet.
  • Job profitability with estimate-to-actual comparisons.
  • Open commitments and unrecorded cost review.
  • Accounts receivable aging, including retainage.
  • Billing backlog and upcoming billing milestones.
  • Short-term cash forecast.
  • Change-order status and margin exceptions.

The best reports focus attention. A steady footing comes from reviewing the same core information on a predictable schedule and assigning action owners before the meeting ends.

Signs the Bookkeeping System Has Been Outgrown

Warning signs include unexplained margin swings, expenses coded without jobs, job reports that do not agree with the books, late billing, growing retainage, missing subcontractor documentation, owner-funded cash gaps, and estimates built from intuition rather than actual historical cost.

The fix is not simply more data entry. It is a connected process from estimate and field activity through purchasing, payroll, billing, collection, reconciliation, and management review.
